Case study 01 · Forecast & Replenishment

A daily push engine that thinks before it ships

Every weekday the engine decides how many units of each fresh product go to each store: one number per store × product, and every number can be traced back, column by column, to the rule that produced it.

Scale300+ stores × ~140 SKUs
CadenceEvery order day
ReplacedExcel / VBA master
StackPython · SQL Server · Excel
The problem

Short shelf life punishes every wrong unit

Fresh food was allocated by a large Excel workbook with VBA macros. It was slow, it broke when someone moved a column, and nobody could explain why a store received 3 units or 30.

With a shelf life of a few days, a unit too many is waste and a unit too few is an empty shelf. Imported products came in a fixed supplier quantity that had to be shared out fairly, every single day.

My role

Rules, code, tests, rollout

I mapped every rule from the workbook and from the people who ran it, rebuilt them as separate Python engines, matched the old output row for row, and only then changed behaviour on purpose, one documented decision at a time.

I also wrote the operating guide, the daily run script and the checks that stop the run when the input data is stale.

The pipeline

Twelve steps, each one a small engine

  1. Freshness gateStops the run if sales or delivery data are missing a working day or look partial.
  2. RangeOnly store × product pairs that are officially in the range; one editable sheet is the source of truth.
  3. Calendar & coverWhich days today's delivery must last: Thursday covers the weekend, Friday covers Monday.
  4. ForecastDemand per weekday from 4 weeks of history, plus a safety buffer by store tier. ML forecast plugged in with a fallback.
  5. New stores & launchesBorrow from neighbours within 1.5 km, from similar products, or from the network average.
  6. Stock from lotsShelf stock rebuilt from delivery lots with expiry dates, sales consumed first-in first-out.
  7. OrderOrder = forecast − stock, never below zero.
  8. Sell-throughLast 7 days: sold most of what was sent → a bit more; sold little → trim the buffer only.
  9. Anti-waste capNever send more than the store can sell before the product expires.
  10. Supplier allocationShort supply: cut where it hurts least. Surplus: share by days of cover, not by headcount.
  11. Shelf presenceOne unit only where the product expired on the shelf and nothing else is coming.
  12. Rule auditA dozen business rules checked on the final output before the file goes out.
Key rules, in plain words

The formulas behind one number

Forecast

Weekday demand plus a tiered buffer

FORECAST = Σ avg(same weekday, 4 wks) + Z × √Σ var
Z: top stores 1.92 · middle 1.64 · low 1.28

Variances are summed, not standard deviations, so a 3-day cover does not inflate the buffer by 70%. That bug existed in the original and was fixed with a before/after test.

Stock

What is really on the shelf

STOCK = delivered (21 d) − sold − expired lots
expired = lots past date, not covered by sales

The system stock counts expired goods still on the books. Rebuilding stock from lots tells the engine what can actually be sold tomorrow.

Anti-waste

Sellable before expiry

stock + order ≤ ⌈daily sales × shelf-life days⌉

Shelf life comes from real delivery and expiry dates (median of the last 3 deliveries), not from master data, which was wrong for most products.

Fair sharing

Surplus by days of cover

next unit → store with the fewest days of cover
cap: stock + sent ≤ 1.5 × sellable

Sharing surplus one unit per store sent goods to stores that don't sell them. Equalising days of cover cut the worst case from weeks of stock to days.

New stores

Neighbours first, then a cap

forecast = max(own sales, neighbours ≤ 1.5 km × 1.3)
after 7 days: ≤ real sales × cover × 1.5

Borrowed numbers never carry the neighbour's buffer. Once a new store has a week of real sales, those sales cap what it receives.

Long-life sibling

Ambient push

need = daily sales × min(days to expiry, target) − stock
best sellers first · shelf-space cap · never above stock

A separate engine for long-life goods: one fixed quantity from the warehouse, shared where it is needed, starting with the strongest stores.

How I know it works

Proof before every change

  • Row-for-row match with the old master on tens of thousands of rows before any deliberate change.
  • Regression snapshots for each weekday: real SQL inputs recorded once, replayed offline after every code change.
  • Rule audit on every run: whole units only, nothing outside the range, never above supplier stock, anti-waste respected.
  • Stock accuracy tracked daily against the system: mean error and bias printed on every run.
  • ML backtest day by day, using only the forecast that existed each morning, before switching it on.
Decisions worth telling

Small rules, big effects

"First sale" was wrong

Launch dates counted only direct sales, so products sold mostly in meal deals looked brand new and got their average divided by too few days. Counting any sale fixed it.

Master data lied

Shelf life in the item master was longer than reality for most products. The engine now reads it from actual delivery and expiry dates.

Non-determinism

The regression test caught an average changing at the second decimal between runs: a set iterated in random order. Every list behind an average is now sorted.

Outcome. One run each order day, one file with the store × product matrix and a full audit sheet behind it. Questions from stores are answered from a single row, and every rule change ships with a before/after comparison.

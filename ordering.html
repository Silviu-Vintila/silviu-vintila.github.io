The problem

Buyers were guessing a week ahead

Every supplier has its own order days, delivery days and number of days each delivery must last. Buyers estimated quantities by hand, with no view of next week's school holiday or of the five stores opening on Thursday.

Too little meant empty shelves for days; too much meant short-life stock that nobody could sell.

My role

Calendar logic, forecast, analysis, training

I designed the calendar reader with the planning team, built the forecast and the holiday analysis behind it, and wrote the manual and slide deck the buyers use.

The proposal reuses the same demand logic as the daily store engine, so the two never disagree about what a store sells.

How a proposal is built

From calendar to case size

  1. Read the calendarOrder day → store delivery day → number of days covered, per supplier.
  2. Check coverageEach supplier's deliveries must cover the week exactly once: no gaps, no double days.
  3. Daily demandSame-weekday average over 4 weeks, zeros included, per store and product.
  4. Store trendLast 14 days vs the 14 before, bounded so one odd week can't swing the order.
  5. Holiday factorLearned per store and category from the most recent comparable holiday.
  6. New openingsFrom the opening date: neighbours within 1.5 km, or the network average.
  7. Sum over coverStore demand × days covered by the delivery, plus a small agreed margin.
  8. Round to MOQSupplier minimum or case multiple; zero stays zero.

The holiday analysis

Learning from every past break

A separate script indexes sales for every past school holiday against the same weekdays around it, per store and per category, and writes the factors the forecast uses.

  • Stores that opened after the last holiday borrow the factors of neighbours within 1.5 km, category by category.
  • No neighbours: the category index for the whole network, not one flat number.
  • Winter and Easter breaks hit fresh sales hard; autumn barely moves them. The order now knows that in advance.
Decisions worth telling

When the data tells a different story

An old holiday was really a launch

One past autumn break coincided with a new ready-meal range going into stores. Its "holiday effect" was a launch effect. Factors now come from the most recent comparable holiday only.

Stock at delivery was a guess

Subtracting projected stock depended on orders already placed, which were themselves estimates. The team chose a demand-plus-margin proposal and keeps projected stock as information.

Range comes from one sheet

New stores get only the products assigned to them, and stores missing from the range sheet are flagged instead of silently receiving a guess.

Outcome. One workbook each morning: summary with warnings, today's orders, the 14-day plan, the daily forecast per product, new stores and the per-store detail. Shipped with a manual and a slide deck so the buying team owns it.

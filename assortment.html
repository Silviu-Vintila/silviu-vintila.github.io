Case study 04 · Assortment & Availability

Same planogram, very different stores: finding out why

A set of analyses for the commercial team: was the product on the shelf, does it sell where it is listed, did a launch add sales or take them from its neighbours, and what happens to each type of store in the summer holiday.

GrainStore × product × day
AudienceCommercial team & board
LanguagesRomanian + English
OutputHTML dashboards + Excel
The question

"Can we really run one planogram everywhere?"

Hundreds of stores shared the same standard range. Averages said the range worked. Nobody could point to the products that sat dead on some shelves, or were missing exactly where they sold best.

New products were launched every few weeks, with no simple way to tell a real success from a product that only took sales from the one next to it.

The foundation

First, know what was on the shelf

Everything rests on one daily fact: was the product on the shelf this morning? I rebuilt it from delivery lots with expiry dates, sales consumed first-in first-out, write-offs taken from expired stock first, and expired lots removed.

AVAILABILITY = days on shelf ÷ days in range
LOST SALES ≈ empty days × average daily sales on shelf days

It was checked against physical stock counts and against the system before anyone used it.

Six signals, each with a list behind it

Method choices

Apples with apples

  • Like-for-like stores for cannibalization and holidays: only stores open before the period, the same stores in every month.
  • Recently opened stores excluded from the planogram review, and the first month after opening never counts.
  • A launch = all new products in the same subcategory and week, compared 4 weeks before vs weeks 2–5 after, with the rest of the range as the seasonal control.
  • Holiday savings are calculated, not assumed: from delisting (losses minus margin) and from orders adjusted to the school-term loss rate.
Decisions worth telling

The first version was wrong, on purpose

Per-product cannibalization

The first cut measured each product alone and produced absurd ratios. Grouping launches by subcategory and week, with filters for products that matter, gave numbers the team could act on.

The 21-day stock formula

Reused from the store engine, it put many sales on days with "zero stock". The availability study switched to a full lot-by-lot simulation from a known starting stock.

One column, not three

Availability was first shown in several scenarios. The team asked for one number, so the most cautious reading became the definition.

Outcome. One run produces the Excel workbook and a self-contained HTML dashboard, in Romanian for the operations team and English for the commercial team. Each signal comes with the stores and products behind it, so the discussion is about a list, not an average.
